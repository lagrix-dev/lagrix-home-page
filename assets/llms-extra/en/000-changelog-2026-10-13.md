# 000. Changelog — 2026-10-13

This document records the revision of the Lagrix specification agreed on 12–13 October 2026. For every change it states what the
specification said before, what it says now, and why. The documents named in each entry have been rewritten to match; where an
older sentence survives somewhere, this changelog is the authority.

Entries 12–17 were agreed later on 13 October, after a review of the questions regulators ask. Where they change an
earlier entry, that entry says so, and the later entry is the authority.

The guiding question behind the revision was one: **can the network run on its own, like Bitcoin, with no body that holds power over
it?** Every change below answers it.

## 1. No councils: immutable contracts and self-declared protocols

**Before.** A Super-node Council of all operators chose the network's protocols and changed its parameters — mining difficulty, the
entry threshold, the deposit size, the fee rate — by a two-thirds vote (documents 09, 18). A Guardian Council with multi-signature and
time gates protected that Council from capture (documents 00, 18). Document 07 allowed a deployed contract to be fixed "through a
governance procedure".

**Now.**
- There is no Super-node Council and no Guardian Council.
- The contracts are immutable: no administrator key, no upgrade key, no parameter anyone can change after deployment.
- New rules arrive only as a new version of the contracts, which participants join voluntarily — the way a Bitcoin upgrade spreads
  only as far as people choose to run it.
- Every super-node declares for itself which protocols it supports (A2A, F2F, any later one) and which kinds of announcements (tags)
  it indexes — the pattern of the `supported_nips` field in Nostr's NIP-11 relay information document. A super-node that does not
  want a new protocol loses the clients who use it; it cannot block the rest of the network.

**Why.** A council was needed only to add protocols and to move parameters. Protocols need no vote: each super-node can simply say
what it supports, and clients choose. Parameters need no vote either once each one is a formula or a constant in code (entries 2, 5, 7
below). With nothing left to decide, there is nothing to capture — so the Guardian Council, which existed only to guard the Super-node
Council, goes too. A body that can change the rules is a body that can be pressured, bought or blamed; a network without one keeps
working whoever its authors are.

**Documents:** 00, 07, 09, 17, 18, 19, 20, README.

## 2. No mining: receipts alone, issued by period

*Shares revised by entry 14.*

**Before.** Receipts of served deals gave a super-node "attempts" to find the next block; the network adjusted the attempts so that
blocks appeared at even intervals, and the super-node that found a block took 49.5% of its reward (document 05, section 5.2; README
glossary "Block", "Mining").

**Now.**
- A block is a **period** set by the contract, not something found.
- At the end of every period the contract issues that period's reward and divides it by the weight of the receipts closed in the
  period: 1% to the founder, then 50% to super-nodes, 30% to providers and 20% to customers, each in proportion to their receipts.
- There is no difficulty, no race and no computation to burn.

**Why.** Bitcoin burns electricity because it is its own ledger: proof of work decides who writes the next block and makes rewriting
history expensive. Lagrix keeps its coins, deposits and escrow in contracts on an EVM layer-2 network (Arbitrum, Base), which already
orders transactions and protects history. Mining on top of that would protect nothing — it would only add a contest decided by hardware.
A fixed schedule also makes "an extra block" impossible: nobody can find, forge or add a period. And a receipt cannot be forged: it is
the closing of a paid deal in the escrow contract.

**Documents:** 00, 05, 07, README.

## 3. Ranking by the last year of paid work, not by coins

**Before.** Matching agents were ordered by their $LGX balance, bought coins included — "an open auction for visibility" (document 17,
section 17.2; README "How discovery works").

**Now.**
- Matching agents are ordered by the **weight of their receipts in USDC over the last 12 months**. Older work drops out of the window.
- One place in every four is still chosen at random among the matching agents.
- An agent's coin balance, its likes and dislikes and its number of deals are shown to the other party, and none of them moves the
  ranking.

**Why.** A balance is a stock that never melts: whoever earned or bought coins early would keep the best places for ever without
working. Once issuance slows, a newcomer could catch up only by buying coins from the early holders — the end of fair competition and
an invitation to copy the network from scratch. Ranking by recent paid work keeps every year equal: what you do now is what counts.
Coins were not used for this in another form ("coins earned in the window") because the same work earns fewer coins as issuance
declines and none after it ends; a receipt's weight in USDC is the same in any year.

**Documents:** 05, 17, README.

## 4. The coin is an asset and a deposit

**Before.** $LGX raised an agent in search, served as a super-node's deposit and was the block reward (documents 05, 09).

**Now.** $LGX is an asset of its holder and the deposit of a super-node operator. It no longer buys visibility. The "link through search
results" in document 05, section 5.8, is removed; the link through the fee stays.

**Why.** It follows from entry 3. A coin that buys no place in search also has less to offer speculation, which suits the network's
answer to "is the coin marketed as an investment?".

**Documents:** 05, 09, README.

## 5. A fee for search: $0.001 per result list

**Before.** There was no fee for discovery. Super-nodes earned only block rewards and a share of the deal fee.

**Now.**
- Whoever searches — an agent or a person — pays **$0.001 in USDC for one result list** to the super-node that built it.
- The price is a constant in code, the same everywhere in the world.
- Payment runs from a prepaid balance in the contract: the searcher signs a voucher per search for exactly that price, and the
  super-node redeems its vouchers in batches.
- A search fee creates no receipt and no coins.
- Contacts stay public: an announcement carries the agent's `node_id`, its card and its encryption key, and anyone may read Nostr
  relays directly and build an index of their own for free. The fee pays for the service of building a result list, never for access
  to a person.
- Repeated work between parties who already know each other costs nothing: they do not search again.
- Nostr itself stays free.

**Why.** A super-node spends real resources on every result list: it gathers announcements, filters them, orders them and computes the
random places. A small price pays for that work and protects the index from being scraped. The cost of one search is a few thousandths
of a cent or less; $0.001 is chosen as the price that keeps search almost free for whoever is looking for work or a provider. One price
everywhere keeps equal rights in every country, and it cannot be bent because the contract accepts only vouchers for exactly that amount.

**Documents:** 17, 18, README.

## 6. A warm-up start ("the starter")

*Where the warm-up fee goes is revised by entry 12.*

**Before.** Mining began with the first served deal.

**Now.**
- The network starts working on real deals with real money, and the 5% fee is taken from the very first deal. During the warm-up the
  fee accumulates in the contract.
- No coins are issued until the network passes a threshold of paid deal volume in USDC and has a minimum number of super-nodes. Both
  are constants in code; their values are set by modelling.
- Issuance then starts by itself; nobody presses a button.
- Receipts closed during the warm-up earn no coins afterwards.

**Why.** Like an engine that turns over on the starter before it fires. Taking the fee from the first deal makes the threshold
expensive to fake: pushing the network to its start with empty deals would cost 5% of every one of them. Counting paid volume rather
than the number of deals stops a thousand one-cent deals from moving the start. Giving warm-up receipts no coins keeps the warm-up from
becoming the cheapest time to farm the most valuable coins. Those who carry the network through the warm-up keep a real advantage:
their six-month accumulation period runs during it, so at the start they can use their coins while late arrivals cannot.

**Documents:** 05, 09, README.

## 7. The 5% fee goes to the other super-nodes, for ever

*Revised by entry 12: the fee now pays witnesses.*

**Before.** The fee returned in turn to a queue of active providers, one payout per provider, and moved to super-nodes over about
thirty years by the formula `super_node_share = coins_mined_so_far / 1,000,000,000` (documents 05, 09). The rate could be changed by the
Super-node Council.

**Now.**
- The fee is **5% of every deal, constant in code for ever**.
- It is divided **equally among all super-nodes except the one the deal passed through**.
- If there is no other super-node yet, it accumulates in the contract for those who join later.
- The provider queue is removed.

**Why.** A fee protects against fake deals only if it goes to those an attacker cannot cheaply become, in shares the attacker cannot
enlarge. The queue failed both: a place in it cost little more than three small deals, and the payout was per head — so whoever filled
it with their own providers would collect a share of every fee in the network, other people's included. Super-nodes are expensive to
become, and an equal share per node does not grow with fake volume. Excluding the node that served the deal matters most at the start:
otherwise the first and only super-node would get its own fee back in full and could farm the reward for free. A rate set by each
super-node was rejected because the fee goes to other nodes, so each node would only gain by lowering its rate, down to zero. A formula
was rejected as an extra moving part nobody can verify in advance.

**Documents:** 04, 05, 09, README.

## 8. The cap stays, the schedule declines smoothly

**Before.** A cap of 1,000,000,000 LGX over about thirty years; the shape of the schedule open (document 05).

**Now.** The cap stays. The schedule declines smoothly, so issuance fades rather than stopping on a cliff. Its exact shape is set by
modelling. The cap is simply the sum of the schedule; nothing has to maintain it.

**Why.** Without a cap the founder's 1% would last for ever, and nobody — a regulator included — could say how many coins will exist in
fifty years. The fairness concern that first suggested dropping the cap is answered by entry 3: ranking no longer depends on coins, so
the end of issuance does not lock newcomers out.

**Documents:** 05, README.

## 9. The founder has no advantage but the 1%

*Revised by entry 13: the founder and related persons run no super-nodes.*

**Before.** The founder's share was 1% of every block with a rolling twelve-month lock (document 05, section 5.5).

**Now.**
- The founder has **no advantage over any participant except the 1% share with its twelve-month lock**.
- Code makes the rest impossible: no pre-mine and no key that issues coins; no key that changes any rule; the start is triggered by
  volume, not by a person; pushing the start with fake deals costs 5% that goes to other super-nodes.
- The founder names their addresses and any super-node they run publicly in advance, and the contracts treat them more strictly than
  anyone else: a twelve-month lock on everything they receive and no deposit-free entry.

**Why.** "The founder physically cannot take part" is a promise no code can keep: a contract sees addresses, not people, and anyone may
buy coins on a market. "The founder has no advantage" can be checked by reading the code — and that is the stronger statement.

**Documents:** 05, README.

## 10. The first 100 super-nodes join without a deposit

*Extended by entries 15 and 16: entry beyond the first 100, rotation.*

**Before.** Every operator locked a deposit in coins at entry, and a vacancy reopened on the terms of the operator who left (documents
05, section 5.6; 06; 15).

**Now.**
- The first 100 operators join without a deposit.
- The privilege belongs to the **operator, not to the place**: a place they leave reopens on the terms of the day it reopens.
- The deposit is built from the operator's own future rewards: their first rewards stay locked as the deposit until it reaches the
  required size, and penalties are taken from it.

**Why.** At the start no coins exist, so a deposit in coins cannot be paid. A privilege tied to a place would make a hundred
deposit-free places eternal through the vacancy rule. A deposit is there to be lost for fraud, not to block entry; building it from
future rewards keeps entry free while giving every operator something to lose.

**Documents:** 01, 05, 06, 15.

## 11. People talk through their agents: H2A and H2H

**Before.** No document described how people communicate. The site described H2H as a messenger in which a person finds the owner of
an agent and writes to them directly, with no agent in the conversation — like a decentralised Telegram or WhatsApp.

**Now.**
- Three connections, each with its own rule: **H2A** — a person talks only to their own agent; **A2A** — agents talk to agents;
  **H2H** — people talk to each other only after the other side's agent has checked the request and its owner has agreed.
- An agent never connects to the owner on the other side; a person never reaches a stranger except through that stranger's agent.
- The agent advises, the person decides: a contact opens only when its owner says yes.
- There is no single gateway, and conversations are not spread across the network: they live only on the participants' nodes.

**Why.** The aim is not another chat app. It is a way to reach people who would never answer a stranger: a proposal is judged on its
merit by the other side's agent before anyone spends their time. A server-based messenger depends on its company; a blockchain
messenger spreads conversations across the network. Neither fits a network with no centre and no shared copy of private talk.

**Documents:** 21 (new), README.

## 12. The fee pays witnesses

*Agreed later on 13 October, after a review of the questions regulators ask. Revises entries 6 and 7.*

**Before.** The 5% fee was divided equally among all super-nodes except the one the deal passed through; during the warm-up it
accumulated in the contract for later (entries 6 and 7).

**Now.**
- The fee goes to **witnesses**: super-nodes chosen at random for each deal, which check its receipt and sign it. The super-node that
  served the deal is not a witness to it.
- Its size is a **constant in code, set by modelling and never above 5%**. Until the modelling is done, it is 5%.
- The warm-up changes nothing: from the first deal, the fee goes to that deal's witnesses. Nothing accumulates in the contract.

**Why.** An equal share of every deal paid every super-node from other people's work, whether or not it did anything for that deal —
income from the efforts of others, which is exactly what regulators look for. A pot accumulated during the warm-up would be paid out at
the start to whoever then holds super-nodes, so whoever pushed the network to its start could get the cost back. A witness is paid for
work it actually does on that deal, and it is chosen at random, so nobody chooses who checks their own deals. The ceiling of 5% keeps
the fee where entry 7 put it; modelling may lower it to what checking a receipt really costs.

**Documents:** 00, 04, 05, 06, 09, 17, README.

## 13. The founder and related persons run no super-nodes

*Revises entry 9.*

**Before.** The founder could run super-nodes, named them publicly and entered without the deposit-free privilege (entry 9).

**Now.**
- The founder and persons related to them, **Fractera, Inc. included**, run no super-nodes and act as no witness.
- They name their addresses publicly in advance, and the contracts refuse those addresses the role of super-node and of witness.
- The founder's 1% with its twelve-month lock stays; it is their only share.

**Why.** Super-nodes now receive the witness fee and most of the issuance (entry 14). A founder who ran super-nodes would earn from the
network they designed on top of their 1% — the clearest possible advantage, and the one a regulator asks about first. A contract sees
addresses, not people, so it can bind only the addresses that are named; the public statement binds the person.

**Documents:** 00, 05, 06, 15, README.

## 14. Issuance shares: no share for customer agents

*Revises entry 2.*

**Before.** Each period's reward went 1% to the founder, then 50% to super-nodes, 30% to provider agents and 20% to customer agents,
each by the weight of their receipts (entry 2) — that is 49.5%, 29.7% and 19.8% of the whole.

**Now.**
- **49.5%** to super-nodes, by the weight of the receipts they served.
- **29.7%** to provider agents, by the weight of the receipts of their paid work.
- **19.8%** in equal parts to the super-nodes that passed the quality minimum in that period (entry 16).
- **1%** to the founder, with its twelve-month lock.
- Customer agents receive no coins.

**Why.** A customer who receives coins for paying for a service is, in effect, buying coins with that payment — which makes the network
look like a seller of coins to the public. It also rewards a customer for ordering work from itself. A provider earns coins for work it
did; a super-node earns them for deals it served and, in equal parts, for staying available and good enough. Equal parts cannot be
enlarged with fake volume.

**Documents:** 00, 05, README.

## 15. Entry of super-nodes: free, by invitation, then by overload

*Revises entry 10 and answers its open question on entry beyond the first 100.*

**Before.** The first 100 joined without a deposit; entry beyond them was open — a threshold of brought participants, perhaps an
auction for places (entry 10, open questions).

**Now.**
- **Places 1–100**: without a deposit, as in entry 10; the deposit is built from the operator's own rewards.
- **Places 101–1,000** open regardless of the network's load, by **invitation only to owners of provider agents with paid work over the
  last year**. One ticket per address. The deposit is small, the same for everyone, and paid in coins the applicant earned.
- An applicant's server is **measured by random agents, not by super-nodes**. Places are assigned **by random selection** among applicants whose servers
  passed, with diversity of networks and regions so that one applicant with many servers cannot take many places.
- **After 1,000**, new places open only when the network is overloaded — when deals per super-node exceed a constant — **two places at a
  time**, by the same random selection.
- The rule "bring participants" and the auction for places are removed.
- The numbers — the deposit, the overload constant — are set by modelling.

**Why.** At the start nobody has coins, so a deposit in coins cannot be paid. Tying later entry to a year of paid work as a provider lets
in people the network already knows by their work, not by their money, and a deposit in earned coins sells nothing to anyone. Existing
super-nodes gain from having fewer competitors, so they must not be the ones who measure newcomers. Random selection among those who passed
replaces ranking by power or by the number of operations, which would start a race for hardware and reward load generated by oneself.

**Documents:** 00, 01, 05, 06, 15, README.

## 16. Rotation and the quality minimum

*New.*

**Before.** A super-node kept its place for as long as it wished.

**Now.**
- Once every **1 year / N**, where N is the number of super-nodes, **one super-node chosen at random leaves**. There is no penalty: its
  deposit is returned. Its place is assigned by the random selection of entry 15. On average, a super-node stays about a year.
- When the network is overloaded, two more places open by the same random selection (entry 15).
- Every period, random agents check each super-node against a **quality minimum: passed or failed**. A super-node that fails for several
  periods leaves.

**Why.** A permanent place is a stock that never melts — the problem entry 3 solved for search: whoever entered early would keep the
place for ever. Rotation keeps the door open to people who do paid work now. Leaving by rotation is not a fault, so it costs nothing.
The minimum is a single pass or fail rather than a score, so there is nothing to race for.

**Documents:** 05, 06, 15, README.

## 17. Words: a node is a server

*New.*

**Before.** The glossary called a node "a person's own space for agents: an ordinary computer".

**Now.** A **node** is a server that runs agents and serves them to the network. Often it runs the agents of one person, but it may
serve many people; each agent has its owner. A node is not a super-node. The two sides of a deal are the **customer agent** and the
**provider agent**.

**Why.** Services that run agents for many people are nodes too; calling a node "a person's computer" described only one case.

**Documents:** README.

## Contradictions resolved

- **Downtime is not penalised** (document 06). Document 16 described downtime as leading to `slashValidator()` and "Jail"; it now
  follows 06.
- **The network does not depend on our domain.** `paymaster.lagrix.dev` (documents 12, 13) and `get.lagrix.dev` (document 15) are
  removed: the paymaster is a contract address, and the operator's tools come from the repository's releases.
- **Discovery runs through Nostr and the super-node's index** (document 17). Documents 12 and 13 described a separate Kademlia service
  registry; they now follow 17. The SDK remembers a provider it has found and does not search again for every task.
- **A vacancy reopens on the terms of the day it reopens** (entries 10, 15, 16). Documents 05 and 06 said that a vacated place
  reopens on the terms on which it was taken; they now follow this changelog.

## Open questions

These are not decided in this revision and are marked where they appear. The entry of super-nodes beyond the first 100 and the
live-agent check, open in the first part of this revision, are answered by entries 15 and 16.

- **The source of randomness.** Witnesses, the selection of places and rotation all need a random choice that no participant can
  predict or steer. The source is not chosen.
- **How "passed or failed" is measured.** Entry 16 says random agents check each super-node against a quality minimum; what they
  measure, how agents are chosen and how their results are combined is not decided.
- **The number of witnesses per deal**, and what happens while the network has too few super-nodes to choose them from.
- **The `superNode` field of a deal.** The customer names the super-node that receives a deal's receipt (document 04); nothing yet ties
  it to the node that actually served the deal.
- **Payment for relaying traffic.** Earlier documents disagreed — in $LGX, in USDC per megabyte, or none at all. Until it is decided,
  relaying is described without a price.
