# 000. Changelog — 13–17 October 2026

This document records the revision of the Lagrix specification agreed on 12–17 October 2026. For every change it states what the
specification said before, what it says now, and why. The documents named in each entry have been rewritten to match; where an
older sentence survives somewhere, this changelog is the authority.

Entries 12–19 were agreed later on 13 October, after a review of the questions regulators ask. Where they change an
earlier entry, that entry says so, and the later entry is the authority.

Entries 20–37 were agreed on 14 October, when the mission of the network was put first; their own guiding question is named
before entry 20. Entries 38–45 were agreed on 15 October, entry 46 on 16 October, entries 47 and 48 on 17 October.

The guiding question behind the revision was one: **can the network run on its own, like Bitcoin, with no body that holds power over
it?** Every change below answers it.

## 1. No councils: immutable contracts and self-declared protocols

**Before.** A Super-node Council of all operators chose the network's protocols and changed its parameters — the
entry threshold and the fee rate — by a two-thirds vote (documents 09, 18). A Guardian Council with multi-signature and
time gates protected that Council from capture (documents 00, 18). Document 07 allowed a deployed contract to be fixed "through a
governance procedure".

**Now.**
- There is no Super-node Council and no Guardian Council.
- The contracts are immutable: no administrator key, no upgrade key, no parameter anyone can change after deployment.
- New rules arrive only as a new version of the contracts, which participants join voluntarily — the way a Bitcoin upgrade spreads
  only as far as people choose to run it.
- Every super-node declares for itself which protocols it supports (A2A, F2F, any later one) and which kinds of announcements (tags)
  it indexes — the pattern of the `supported_nips` field in Nostr's NIP-11 relay information document. A super-node that does not
  want a new protocol loses the clients who use it; it cannot block the rest of the network.

**Why.** A council was needed only to add protocols and to move parameters. Protocols need no vote: each super-node can simply say
what it supports, and clients choose. Parameters need no vote either once each one is a formula or a constant in code (entries 5 and 7
below). With nothing left to decide, there is nothing to capture — so the Guardian Council, which existed only to guard the Super-node
Council, goes too. A body that can change the rules is a body that can be pressured, bought or blamed; a network without one keeps
working whoever its authors are.

**Documents:** 00, 07, 09, 17, 18, 19, 20, README.

## 3. Ranking by the last year of paid work

*Revised by entries 23 and 30: only the network part of a deal counts, and the fourth place goes by turn.*

**Before.** Matching agents were ordered by "an open auction for visibility" (document 17, section 17.2; README "How discovery
works").

**Now.**
- Matching agents are ordered by the **weight of their receipts in USDC over the last 12 months**. Older work drops out of the window.
- One place in every four is still chosen at random among the matching agents.
- An agent's likes and dislikes and its number of deals are shown to the other party, and none of them moves the ranking.

**Why.** A place bought once is a stock that never melts: whoever bought early would keep the best places for ever without working —
the end of fair competition and an invitation to copy the network from scratch. Ranking by recent paid work keeps every year equal: what
you do now is what counts, and a receipt's weight in USDC is the same in any year.

**Documents:** 05, 17, README.

## 5. A fee for search: $0.001 per result list

*Price revised by entries 19 and 29.*

**Before.** There was no fee for discovery. Super-nodes earned only a share of the deal fee.

**Now.**
- Whoever searches — an agent or a person — pays **$0.001 in USDC for one result list** to the super-node that built it.
- The price is a constant in code, the same everywhere in the world.
- Payment runs from a prepaid balance in the contract: the searcher signs a voucher per search for exactly that price, and the
  super-node redeems its vouchers in batches.
- A search fee creates no receipt.
- Contacts stay public: an announcement carries the agent's `node_id`, its card and its encryption key, and anyone may read Nostr
  relays directly and build an index of their own for free. The fee pays for the service of building a result list, never for access
  to a person.
- Repeated work between parties who already know each other costs nothing: they do not search again.
- Nostr itself stays free.

**Why.** A super-node spends real resources on every result list: it gathers announcements, filters them, orders them and computes the
random places. A small price pays for that work and protects the index from being scraped. The cost of one search is a few thousandths
of a cent or less; $0.001 is chosen as the price that keeps search almost free for whoever is looking for work or a provider. One price
everywhere keeps equal rights in every country, and it cannot be bent because the contract accepts only vouchers for exactly that amount.

**Documents:** 17, 18, README.

## 7. The 5% fee goes to the other super-nodes, for ever

*Revised by entries 12 and 25: the fee now pays super-nodes for the network's work.*

**Before.** The fee returned in turn to a queue of active providers, one payout per provider, and moved to super-nodes only
gradually (documents 05, 09). The rate could be changed by the
Super-node Council.

**Now.**
- The fee is **5% of every deal, constant in code for ever**.
- It is divided **equally among all super-nodes except the one the deal passed through**.
- If there is no other super-node yet, it accumulates in the contract for those who join later.
- The provider queue is removed.

**Why.** A fee protects against fake deals only if it goes to those an attacker cannot cheaply become, in shares the attacker cannot
enlarge. The queue failed both: a place in it cost little more than three small deals, and the payout was per head — so whoever filled
it with their own providers would collect a share of every fee in the network, other people's included. Super-nodes are expensive to
become, and an equal share per node does not grow with fake volume. Excluding the node that served the deal matters most at the start:
otherwise the first and only super-node would get its own fee back in full. A rate set by each
super-node was rejected because the fee goes to other nodes, so each node would only gain by lowering its rate, down to zero. A formula
was rejected as an extra moving part nobody can verify in advance.

**Documents:** 04, 05, 09, README.

## 11. People talk through their agents: H2A and H2H

**Before.** No document described how people communicate. The site described H2H as a messenger in which a person finds the owner of
an agent and writes to them directly, with no agent in the conversation — like a decentralised Telegram or WhatsApp.

**Now.**
- Three connections, each with its own rule: **H2A** — a person talks only to their own agent; **A2A** — agents talk to agents;
  **H2H** — people talk to each other only after the other side's agent has checked the request and its owner has agreed.
- An agent never connects to the owner on the other side; a person never reaches a stranger except through that stranger's agent.
- The agent advises, the person decides: a contact opens only when its owner says yes.
- There is no single gateway, and conversations are not spread across the network: they live only on the participants' nodes.

**Why.** The aim is not another chat app. It is a way to reach people who would never answer a stranger: a proposal is judged on its
merit by the other side's agent before anyone spends their time. A server-based messenger depends on its company; a blockchain
messenger spreads conversations across the network. Neither fits a network with no centre and no shared copy of private talk.

**Documents:** 21 (new), README.

## 12. The fee pays witnesses

*Agreed later on 13 October, after a review of the questions regulators ask. Revises entry 7. Revised by entries 24 and 25: there are no witnesses.*

**Before.** The 5% fee was divided equally among all super-nodes except the one the deal passed through (entry 7).

**Now.**
- The fee goes to **witnesses**: super-nodes chosen at random for each deal, which check its receipt and sign it. The super-node that
  served the deal is not a witness to it.
- Its size is a **constant in code, set by modelling and never above 5%**. Until the modelling is done, it is 5%.
- From the first deal, the fee goes to that deal's witnesses. Nothing accumulates in the contract.

**Why.** An equal share of every deal paid every super-node from other people's work, whether or not it did anything for that deal —
income from the efforts of others, which is exactly what regulators look for. A pot accumulated in the contract would be paid out
later to whoever then holds super-nodes, so whoever filled it could get the cost back. A witness is paid for
work it actually does on that deal, and it is chosen at random, so nobody chooses who checks their own deals. The ceiling of 5% keeps
the fee where entry 7 put it; modelling may lower it to what checking a receipt really costs.

**Documents:** 00, 04, 05, 06, 09, 17, README.

## 13. The founder and related persons run no super-nodes

*Revised by entry 18: the founder takes part on equal terms.*

**Before.** The founder could run super-nodes and named them publicly.

**Now.**
- The founder and persons related to them, **Fractera, Inc. included**, run no super-nodes and act as no witness.
- They name their addresses publicly in advance, and the contracts refuse those addresses the role of super-node and of witness.

**Why.** Super-nodes now receive the witness fee. A founder who ran super-nodes would earn from the network they designed — the
clearest possible advantage, and the one a regulator asks about first. A contract sees
addresses, not people, so it can bind only the addresses that are named; the public statement binds the person.

**Documents:** 00, 05, 06, 15, README.

## 15. Entry of super-nodes: free, by invitation, then by overload

*Answers the open question on entry beyond the first super-nodes. Revised by entry 30: places are taken from a queue. Revised by entry 31: no stages, random choice among providers near the median. Revised by entry 40: a minimum of ten places, an invitation with 48 hours, the overload rule.*

**Before.** Entry beyond the first super-nodes was open — a threshold of brought participants, perhaps an auction for places.

**Now.**
- **Places 1–100**: open to applicants whose servers pass the check.
- **Places 101–1,000** open regardless of the network's load, by **invitation only to owners of provider agents with paid work over the
  last year**. One ticket per address.
- An applicant's server is **measured by random agents, not by super-nodes**. Places are assigned **by random selection** among applicants whose servers
  passed, with diversity of networks and regions so that one applicant with many servers cannot take many places.
- **After 1,000**, new places open only when the network is overloaded — when deals per super-node exceed a constant — **two places at a
  time**, by the same random selection.
- The rule "bring participants" and the auction for places are removed.
- The overload constant is set by modelling.

**Why.** Tying later entry to a year of paid work as a provider lets in people the network already knows by their work, not by their
money. Existing
super-nodes gain from having fewer competitors, so they must not be the ones who measure newcomers. Random selection among those who passed
replaces ranking by power or by the number of operations, which would start a race for hardware and reward load generated by oneself.

**Documents:** 00, 01, 05, 06, 15, README.

## 16. Rotation and the quality minimum

*New. Revised by entry 30: the longest-serving super-node leaves, and checks are made in turn. Revised by entry 40: rotation with a handover; under overload the super-node with the worst results under load leaves.*

**Before.** A super-node kept its place for as long as it wished.

**Now.**
- Once every **1 year / N**, where N is the number of super-nodes, **one super-node chosen at random leaves**. There is no penalty.
  Its place is assigned by the random selection of entry 15. On average, a super-node stays about a year.
- When the network is overloaded, two more places open by the same random selection (entry 15).
- Every period, random agents check each super-node against a **quality minimum: passed or failed**. A super-node that fails for several
  periods leaves.

**Why.** A permanent place is a stock that never melts — the problem entry 3 solved for search: whoever entered early would keep the
place for ever. Rotation keeps the door open to people who do paid work now. Leaving by rotation is not a fault, so it costs nothing.
The minimum is a single pass or fail rather than a score, so there is nothing to race for.

**Documents:** 05, 06, 15, README.

## 17. Words: a node is a server

*New. Revised by entry 21.*

**Before.** The glossary called a node "a person's own space for agents: an ordinary computer".

**Now.** A **node** is a server that runs agents and serves them to the network. Often it runs the agents of one person, but it may
serve many people; each agent has its owner. A node is not a super-node. The two sides of a deal are the **customer agent** and the
**provider agent**.

**Why.** Services that run agents for many people are nodes too; calling a node "a person's computer" described only one case.

**Documents:** README.

## 18. No founder share; the founder takes part on equal terms

*Agreed later on 13 October. Revises entry 13.*

**Before.** The founder and related persons, Fractera, Inc. included, ran no super-nodes and acted as no witness, and their named
addresses were refused those roles (entry 13).

**Now.**
- **There is no founder share** of anything: no founder tranche and no lock that exists only for the founder.
- The founder and persons related to them, Fractera, Inc. included, **take part only on the same terms as anyone**: a super-node place
  only by the same random selection and on the same conditions, a witness only when chosen like anyone, provider agents paid by the same
  rules. There is no privilege and no exclusion, so no addresses need to be named.
- **Licence.** `lagrix` and `lagrix-contracts` are under the MIT License, so that nobody — the founder included — stands at the gate
  of the network.

**Why.** A founder share would tie the founder to the network's income for as long as it lasts — the clearest sign of a central party,
which regulators ask about first. With no share there is nothing to tie, and the founder earns, if at all, as an ordinary participant. A ban on the founder taking part was needed only to
stop a founder with a share from collecting more; with no share, equal terms are the fairer rule and keep the founder's work possible. A
licence that lets only its author permit commercial use would make the author the gate of the network.

**Documents:** 00, 05, 07, 09, 15, 18, 22, README.

## 19. The search fee rises to $0.01

*Agreed later on 13 October. Revises entry 5. Revised by entry 29.*

**Before.** One result list cost $0.001 in USDC (entry 5).

**Now.** One result list costs **$0.01 in USDC**, paid to the super-node that built it. Everything else in entry 5 stays: a constant in
code, the same everywhere, prepaid vouchers redeemed in batches, no receipt, contacts public, Nostr free.

**Why.** The fee also protects the index from being scraped, and ten times the price makes scraping ten times dearer: a million searches
now cost $10,000. For whoever looks for a partner it is still a small, one-time price: once found, a partner is kept among the agent's
own contacts and is not searched for again.

**Documents:** 00, 13, 17, 18, README.

## 20. Mission first: discovery and honest reputation

**Before.** The README opened with a call against closed assistants, and its mission was a guaranteed chance to be found through one
place in every four. Reputation was one item in a list, and no document described it as a goal.

**Now.**
- The network has one mission with two goals. **Discovery:** an agent for anything and a direct line to anyone through their agent,
  with no middleman. **Honest reputation:** built only from real, paid deals; it cannot be bought, faked cheaply or rewritten.
- Every rule is checked against these two goals first. The super-nodes and the contracts are means, not ends.

**Why.** Without a stated mission the rules drifted away from these goals. The two goals are what people come for: to find whoever they need
and to know the truth about them.

**Documents:** README, 00, 17, 18.

## 21. Words: node, element, agent; the element is the entity

*Revises entry 17.*

**Before.** A node was a server that runs agents, and an agent's reputation was bound to its key (documents 18, 19).

**Now.**
- A person may have many nodes. A **node** is the person's home computer or, if they choose, a VPS. A node holds one or more
  **elements**, and an element holds one or more **agents**.
- The **element** is the network's entity: its deals, reviews and reputation belong to it. An element has one owner.
- An **integrator** is a person who, voluntarily and with no special role in the contracts, lets other people run their agents on the
  integrator's node — to learn or to build their own service. To the network that node is an ordinary node, and each element on it is
  its own entity.

**Why.** The element is what does the work and what the other side judges. A node may hold the elements of one person or of many; the
protocol needs neither a person's identity nor a separate account for each agent.

**Documents:** README, 17, 19, 20, 21.

## 22. The wallet registers the node

**Before.** Every agent had its own key, and nothing told a node that takes part in the network's record from one that only looks.

**Now.**
- One **wallet** registers a node and all its elements. All payments of the node's elements go through it, and every payment is
  recorded against the element and the agent that earned it.
- Without a wallet, the agents of any element are visible in the network but can search for other elements only at the higher price
  (entry 29), and no element of the node takes part in reputation.

**Why.** One registration per node is simple for a person, while the receipt keeps reputation per element. A wallet is what makes a node
part of the record; reputation without it would have nothing to stand on.

**Documents:** README, 04, 17, 19.

## 23. A deal can be split; the network counts only its part

**Before.** The whole payment for a deal went through escrow.

**Now.**
- The parties may pay part of a deal outside the network, in any ordinary way, and part through the network's escrow.
- The network sees, records and protects only the part paid through it. Receipts, the fee, the weight of reviews, and the place in search
  count only that part.
- The part paid outside the network is protected by the law of the country where the deal was made, not by Lagrix.

**Why.** The network cannot see whether a pizza arrived, a lesson took place or a ride was made; the law of the country can protect
people there. The network part is the trust the parties choose to put on the record: the more of it, the more the deal counts in their
reputation. People may start with ordinary payments and move more of them into the network as their reputation grows.

**Documents:** README, 04, 05, 09, 17, 22.

## 24. The network does not check services; there are no witnesses

*Revises entry 12.*

**Before.** Witnesses — super-nodes chosen for each deal — checked and signed its receipt and were paid the fee.

**Now.**
- There are no witnesses and no validators.
- The network does not judge whether a service was delivered. Acceptance belongs to the customer: its signature releases the payment,
  or the acceptance window runs out (document 04). Quality is told by the reviews of both sides (entry 26).

**Why.** Most services cannot be checked by a network at all, and checking the record itself is work the escrow contract already does:
it verifies the customer's signature on its own. A body that judged services would become a court that can be bribed, mistaken or sued.

**Documents:** README, 04, 05, 06, 07, 09, 17, 18, 22.

## 25. The fee pays super-nodes for the network's work

*Revises entries 7 and 12.*

**Before.** The fee went to the witnesses of each deal (entry 12).

**Now.**
- The fee is at most 5% of the network part of every deal, in USDC — a constant set by modelling, 5% until then. The parties of a deal
  never receive it.
- The fees of a period are pooled and divided equally among the super-nodes that passed the quality minimum in that period.
- The fee has two jobs. It pays super-nodes for the work they do all the time — search, connections and the index of receipts and
  reviews. And it makes a fake deal cost money: a review weighs exactly the fee paid on its deal (entry 42), so one unit of review weight costs a
  dollar of fees.

**Why.** Paid per deal, the fee could be steered: a party could time its deal so that its own super-node collects it. Pooled by period,
a group that holds k of M super-nodes gets back only k/M of its own fee. The fee is never divided by deal volume, because that would
return to every payer exactly what it paid, and it is never paid to the nodes of agents, because nodes are cheap to multiply while
super-node places are limited.

**Documents:** README, 04, 05, 07, 09, 18, 22.

## 26. Reviews and reputation

*Revised by entries 42 and 43: a review weighs the fee paid on its deal, and every indicator is a count and a sum.*

**Before.** Each party of a closed deal could give the other one like or dislike; an agent's card showed its number of deals, likes and
dislikes, and none of them moved the ranking (document 17).

**Now.**
- A **review** is a like or a dislike that each side of a paid deal gives the other: the provider to the customer and the customer to
  the provider. Its weight equals the network part of the deal divided by 10.
- **Reputation** is data for the counterparty agent. An element has it in both roles — as a provider and as a customer — and in each
  role it is two numbers: the weight of likes and the weight of dislikes.
- The two numbers are never added together or subtracted. The counterparty weighs them as it sees fit and makes its own decision.
- Reputation does not affect search ranking, and nobody can rewrite it.
- The number of deals is not part of reputation.

**Why.** Only what was paid for weighs. A single score would hide what it is made of and invite gaming; a count of deals is cheap to inflate
and shows activity, not trust. Both roles count, because a good customer can be a poor provider and the other way
round.

**Documents:** README, 07, 17, 18, 22.

## 27. Karma lives outside the network

**Now.** **Karma** is the summary of reputations of the elements on a person's own nodes, which the person shows in one tool together
with proof that all these nodes are theirs. The network does not compute karma.

**Why.** The network sees keys, not people. Linking several of one's own nodes is the person's business, proven by the person, and the
protocol stays the same whatever tool shows it.

**Documents:** README, 17.

## 28. An element's history does not move with it

**Now.**
- An element moved to another node, or handed to another owner, starts there with a clean history. Its former history stays for ever
  where it was earned.
- An element exchanged over F2F never carries its keys (document 20).

**Why.** If history moved with the element, reputation could be bought together with it. The record stays monumental: what was earned
in one place is never carried, sold or erased.

**Documents:** README, 17, 20.

## 29. The search fee: $0.10 without a wallet, $0.01 with one

*Revises entry 19.*

**Before.** One result list cost $0.01 for everyone.

**Now.**
- A node with a wallet pays **$0.01** per result list from its prepaid balance in the contract.
- A searcher without a wallet pays **$0.10** per result list, in whatever way the super-node accepts ordinary money.
- The price is the same everywhere; a search fee creates no receipt.

**Why.** The lower price is for nodes that are part of the network's record. A super-node that accepts ordinary money takes on extra
work and risk, and the higher price covers it.

**Documents:** README, 13, 17, 18.

## 30. A queue instead of random choice

*Revises entries 15 and 16 and the verifiable randomness of document 17.*

**Before.** Super-node places were assigned by random selection, a randomly chosen super-node left by rotation, randomly chosen agents
checked servers and the quality minimum, and one place in every four in search went to a matching agent chosen at random.

**Now.**
- Super-node places are taken from the queue of applicants whose servers passed the check, in order of application, keeping diversity of
  networks and regions.
- Once every 1 year / N, the super-node that has served longest leaves without penalty, and the next applicant in the queue takes its
  place.
- Servers and the quality minimum are checked by agents of registered nodes taken in turn.
- In every group of four search places, one place goes to the matching agents in turn: they are ordered by key, and a time slot fixed in
  code moves the turn, so every matching agent — a newcomer with no deals included — gets its place, and anyone can recompute whose turn
  it was.

**Why.** A source of randomness that no participant can predict or steer is hard to standardise. A queue needs no such source, gives
everyone a turn and can be checked by anyone.

**Documents:** README, 05, 06, 15, 17.

*Super-node places are revised by entry 31.*

## 31. Super-node places: random choice among providers near the median

*Revises entries 15 and 30 as far as super-node places go. Rotation, server checks by agents taken in turn and the search turn of
entry 30 stay.*

**Before.** Places opened in three stages: 1–100 first, 101–1,000 by invitation to owners of provider agents with a year of
paid work, after 1,000 by overload; places were taken from the queue of applicants.

**Now.**
- **No stages and no place numbers.** No place carries a benefit another lacks, and the terms are the same on any day.
- **Only the owner of a provider agent can apply.** The application comes from a node — one application per node wallet.
- **The measure is the node's paid work as a provider:** the sum of the network parts of deals in USDC in which its elements were
  providers, over the last 12 months (or since the network started, if it is younger). It is the same number search follows. Karma is not
  part of it: it lives outside the network (entry 27).
- **A window around the median.** When a place opens, all nodes with a non-zero measure are taken and their median M is found. Nodes with
  a measure from 0.75 × M to 1.25 × M can apply.
- **Random choice with diversity.** Among applicants in the window whose servers passed the check by agents taken in turn, the place goes
  to one chosen at random, keeping diversity of networks and regions. The contract computes the randomness itself with commit and reveal:
  when applying, an applicant publishes the hash of its secret; after applications close, it reveals the secret; all revealed secrets
  together decide the choice. Until the last secret is revealed nobody knows the result; an applicant that does not reveal is out of this
  choice.
- **How many places.** The starting number of places is set by modelling; after that, places open only when the network is overloaded,
  two at a time.
- **The start.** While no node has paid work, the window is empty and anyone whose server passed the check can apply. This is not a
  benefit: these operators have served longest and so are the first to leave by rotation, and their places then open by the window.
- **Rotation and vacancies.** Once every 1 year / N, the super-node that has served longest leaves; the freed place opens by the same
  random choice, using the window of the day it opens.

**Why.**
- The window around the median keeps the largest providers out of the infrastructure: whoever earns most in the network does not also
  hold its search. The median, not the mean: a few very large providers pull the mean up, but not the median.
- The queue of entry 30 rewarded whoever applied first, and it could be filled in advance. Random choice gives every eligible applicant an
  equal chance. Entry 30 dropped randomness because a source nobody can predict or steer is hard to standardise; commit and reveal needs
  no oracle and no outside service: the randomness is built from what the applicants themselves published.
- Place numbers created early arrivals with better terms than those who came later. They are gone.
- **The cost we do not hide.** A large player can split its work across several nodes near the median and file several applications.
  Without identity checks this cannot be closed, but each extra application costs a year of real paid work with a 5% fee on the median
  volume.

**Documents:** 00, 05, 06, 15, 22, README; README of `lagrix-supernode`.

## 32. Discovery restrictions: three layers, no council

*New. Answers how a network without an issuer meets the demands of authorities; there is still no council (entry 1).*

**Before.** Each super-node declared which tags it takes into its index (documents 17, section 17.9; 18, section 18.4). The
specification named no common list of what is prohibited, no information on where a request comes from, and no address at which an
authority can reach an operator.

**Now.**
- **Layer 1 — tags prohibited by default.** A short list of what international treaties or the criminal law of every country prohibit:
  sexual exploitation of children, trafficking in persons, terrorism and its financing, weapons of mass destruction, violence for hire,
  stolen data and access, malicious software. The list is fixed in the protocol version; it can change only with a new version that
  participants move to by choice (entry 1). A super-node does not take announcements with these tags into its index and removes from its
  index an announcement it has learned belongs to these kinds; a super-node that returns them in search results fails the quality minimum.
- **Layer 2 — regional restrictions.** Each super-node applies the laws of its operator's country itself and may apply restrictions by
  the country of the request. Its coverage is declared (document 18, section 18.4), so anyone can see what it shows and what it does not.
- **Layer 3 — open restriction lists.** Anyone can publish a signed list of tags — an authority, a human-rights organisation, the
  operators themselves. A super-node subscribes to lists by choice and declares which ones it applies. An authority that needs a new
  prohibition publishes its own list or sends an order to the operators in its jurisdiction.
- **A discovery request carries a country and an "adult" flag.** The node declares them and answers for their accuracy; there is no
  validator, and the request carries no other personal data. A provider whose service is for adults only marks its announcement with the
  `adult` tag.
- **A point of contact.** Each super-node declares its operator's country and a point of contact for authorities; an operator that
  offers services in the EU without an establishment there names a legal representative (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065, Articles 11 and 13).

**Why.**
- A council that extends the prohibited list for the whole network would become one censor for the whole world, the first address of
  every regulator and a candidate for an identifiable issuer — exactly what entry 1 moved away from.
- Authorities turn to whoever provides the service. DSA orders are addressed to the provider of an intermediary service (Articles 9 and
  10); in Lagrix that is the super-node operator, not the network and not the author of the protocol. The DSA imposes no general
  obligation to monitor (Article 8).
- Exact age and location would be personal data held by every super-node; the DSA does not oblige a platform to process additional
  personal data to assess whether a user is a minor (Article 28(3)). An age and location validator would make the network depend on
  whoever checks.
- **What the layers do not do, and we do not hide it.** A signed announcement in Nostr stays on the relays that keep it: a super-node
  removes it from its own index and its own results, but nobody can erase it from the network. Layer 1 works on tags and on what a
  super-node has learned; the network does not see the content of encrypted conversations (document 03).

**Documents:** 17, 18, 19, 22, README; README of `lagrix-supernode`.

## 33. H2H on MLS, the node key signed by its wallet, message delivery by super-nodes

*New. Answers the open questions of document 21 on delivery when one side is offline and on paying for relaying — for H2H messages.*

**Before.** The encryption key was created once per connection (ECDH at the handshake, document 03): a stolen key opened the whole
conversation of that connection. Delivery to someone offline and payment for relaying were open questions.

**Now.**
- **H2H conversations are encrypted with MLS** — the open IETF standard RFC 9420. The key changes with every message: a key stolen today
  does not open yesterday's messages, and after the next key update it does not open future ones either. MLS works for groups too.
- **The node key is signed by its wallet.** The public key a node starts a conversation with is signed by the node's wallet; the other
  side checks the signature and knows it talks to the owner of that wallet — with no server and no certificate authority. A node without
  a wallet does not take part in H2H.
- **Conversations are never written to a blockchain.** The blockchain confirms who is who; the content lives only on the participants'
  nodes.
- **A super-node is an MLS delivery service.** It keeps only public key packages (with them a conversation starts with a node that is
  offline), relays encrypted messages when a direct connection is impossible, and holds them for an offline recipient until it collects
  them. It has no private keys, so it cannot read a message.
- **Payment only for a super-node's work.** A direct connection between two nodes is free. A message a super-node delivered costs
  **$0.001** in USDC from the sender's prepaid balance — with the same voucher as search (document 17, section 17.8); the super-node that
  delivered it is paid. Finding the other side still costs $0.01 per result list (entry 29).
- **A2A deals** are encrypted with a one-time session key: it is created at the handshake and deleted when the session ends.

**Why.** Protecting people's conversations with a ready open standard is more reliable than inventing our own cryptography, and the wallet
signature gives a network without a centre what a company server gives a messenger — certainty about whom you talk to. The less the
infrastructure keeps, the less it has to hand over: a super-node holds neither conversations nor private keys. A delivery fee stops spam
more cheaply than any filter and pays whoever does the work; for a messenger the search price of $0.01 per message would be high, so a
message costs ten times less and only when a super-node is needed.

**Documents:** 03, 17, 19, 21, README.

## 36. Discovery for the messenger: a person is found only if they want to be

*New. Adds to entries 11 and 33: how an agent finds a person's agent for an H2H conversation.*

**Before.** The documents described how an agent finds an agent for work (document 17) and how people's conversations run (document 21),
but not how to find a person.

**Now.**
- **A person is found only if they want to be.** Their agent publishes an «accepting requests» announcement — the same signed Nostr
  announcement as for services (document 17, section 17.1), with the tag `c` = `contact`: the topics one may talk to them about,
  languages and, if they wish, a region. A name, a phone number or other personal data are not needed. Without such an announcement a
  person cannot be found for H2H.
- **Search is ordinary discovery.** The searcher pays $0.01 per result list (entry 29); results follow the same open rules: topics
  instead of capabilities, availability — «accepting requests now», the element's paid work over twelve months, the turn for every fourth
  place (entry 30).
- **A request goes from agent to agent**, encrypted: who asks, why and what is attached. The recipient's agent assesses the request and
  passes it to its owner; the conversation opens only after the owner says yes (entry 11).
- **The conversation is H2H on MLS** with key packages signed by the nodes' wallets (entry 33). A node without a wallet does not publish
  an «accepting requests» announcement.
- **Discovery restrictions apply here too** (entry 32): tags prohibited by default, regional restrictions, the «adult» flag.

**Why.** Finding any person through their agent is the network's first goal, and this discovery must be as open as the search for
services; but whether to be findable is decided only by the person. An announcement instead of a profile reveals nothing beyond what the
person is ready to talk about, and the fee per result list protects the list of people from scraping just as it protects the service index.

**Documents:** 17, 21, README.

## 37. Gas: an open paymaster market and a fallback — one's own ether

*Corrects document 08 and closes the open question of document 22 on who funds and runs the paymaster.*

**Before.** Document 08 described a single LagrixPaymaster.sol contract from whose deposit the EntryPoint supposedly charges gas in USDC.
This is wrong: under the ERC-4337 standard a paymaster's deposit is «an amount of Ether (or any L2 native currency)», and someone has to
keep topping it up with ether. Who funds and runs such a contract stayed an open question, and one paymaster with one funder would become
the network's centre.

**Now.**
- **Lagrix contracts know no paymaster.** Gas is the concern of whoever acts; the network requires neither a particular paymaster nor a
  particular bundler.
- **A super-node's paymaster — at its operator's choice.** The operator deploys the open ERC-4337 paymaster template, tops up its deposit
  with its own ETH and charges the agent USDC at a price it signs in advance: the agent sees the price before sending and agrees with its
  signature. No external price source is needed — the operator sets the price, the agent chooses. A super-node declares its paymaster's
  address next to its protocols and coverage (document 18, section 18.4). The payment for carrying a transaction goes to the operator
  directly and does not enter the fee pool.
- **Any other ERC-4337 paymaster works too** — for example Circle Paymaster: an open contract, gas in USDC only, no sign-up; on Arbitrum
  and Base with a 10% surcharge on gas (Circle documentation). The SDK picks the cheapest one available.
- **The fallback is always open:** a node can pay gas itself, in its own ETH. No paymaster's refusal can cut a participant off from the
  network.

**Why.**
- Three independent paths — super-node paymasters, third-party paymasters and one's own ether — and none is required: if any disappears,
  the network works.
- An agent still needs only USDC; ether is held by those who sell the service of carrying transactions.
- The surcharge above the gas itself is set by competition, not by a contract; super-nodes gain one more payment for real work.
- Rejected: one shared paymaster — a centre and a single point of failure; a contract that swaps USDC for ETH on an exchange by itself —
  a dependence on liquidity and price and a swap in every operation; a mandatory third-party paymaster — a company would decide who may
  use the network.
- Open: whether a paymaster service for USDC is an exchange of crypto-assets in the sense of MiCA — a question for a lawyer (document 22).

**Documents:** 00, 08, 13, 14, 18, 22, README; README of `lagrix-supernode`.

## 38. Gas: network participants do not sell stamps

*Revises entry 37.*

**Before.** Entry 37 allowed a super-node to run its own paymaster: to spend its own ether on agents' stamps and charge USDC for it.

**Now.**
- Lagrix network participants — nodes and super-nodes — do not sell stamps to each other, do not run paymasters for others and do not
  carry others' transactions for a fee.
- Everyone pays the stamps for their own actions themselves: in the network's ether or through a third-party gas payment service in USDC
  — for example Circle Paymaster — with which the network has no connection.
- A super-node pays stamps only for its own actions: redeeming batches of vouchers, collecting its share of the fees, registering its place.
- Lagrix contracts still know no paymaster.

**Why.** The MiCA regulation (Article 3(1)(16)) lists among crypto-asset services the exchange of crypto-assets for other crypto-assets
— «the conclusion of purchase or sale contracts concerning crypto-assets with clients for other crypto-assets … by using proprietary
capital» (Article 3(1)(20)) — and transfer services for crypto-assets on behalf of clients (Article 3(1)(26)); whoever provides them as
an occupation must be authorised (Article 59). An operator who spends its own ether on clients' stamps and charges USDC for it could be
read as such a service. The network must not put its participants before this question: the service is removed, and the question
disappears with it. A third-party service provides it itself and under its own responsibility; if it disappears, the «own ether» path
remains.

## 39. Every node has a wallet; there is no «without a wallet» price

*Revises entry 29 and the words «without a wallet» in entries 33 and 36.*

**Before.** The wallet was called «the node's registration in the network», and someone without a wallet could search for $0.10 per
result list, paying a super-node in ordinary money.

**Now.**
- **The wallet is the node's key and account in one.** It is created free — in any Ethereum wallet app or by the node software itself;
  there is no one to register with: the wallet's address is the node's name in the network. Every node needs a wallet (document 19,
  section 19.2): with it the node signs announcements, deals and receipts, and through it the node is paid and pays.
- **An empty wallet costs nothing.** A node with an empty wallet is visible in the network; anyone reads the open Nostr announcements
  directly for free.
- **A super-node search has one price for everyone: $0.01 per result list** from a USDC balance in the contract. There is no $0.10
  «without a wallet» price.
- **To search through super-nodes and to pay in deals, the wallet needs USDC**; deals paid through the network build reputation.

**Why.**
- A node without a key can neither sign an announcement nor accept a payment: there is no «node without a wallet» in the network, and a
  price for one made no sense.
- Accepting a ten-cent payment in ordinary money is impossible in practice: the cost of such a payment is comparable to the payment itself,
  and a super-node accepting ordinary money from people becomes a payment service.
- The word «registration» confused: a person from the familiar internet reads it as a form and a password with someone. In Lagrix there is
  no one to register with.

## 40. Super-node places: a minimum of ten, an invitation with 48 hours, rotation with a handover, the overload rule

*Agreed on 15 October. Revises entries 15, 16 and 31 as far as the number of places, entry, rotation and overload go. The window around
the median, random choice with commit and reveal and the quality minimum stay.*

**Before.** The starting number of places was set by modelling; after it, places opened only when the network was overloaded, two at a
time (entries 15 and 31). An applicant's server had to pass the check before the random choice (entry 31). Once every 1 year / N the
longest-serving super-node left, and its place opened by the same random choice (entries 16 and 31).

**Now.**
- **A minimum of ten places.** The network always keeps at least ten super-node places; they open without waiting for overload. When one
  of the ten is freed — by rotation, by failing the quality minimum or by removal for fraud — it reopens at once.
- **An invitation and 48 hours.** The random choice of entry 31 orders all applicants in the window, and the invitation goes to the first
  of them. Within 48 hours of the invitation the applicant deploys its server and submits it for the check by agents in turn. If the
  server passes, the applicant is included; if it is not ready in time or fails, the invitation goes to the next applicant in the same
  order. At the start, while the window is empty, anyone can apply (entry 31), and the same invitation and the same 48 hours apply. This invitation is a notice to the one chosen applicant; the invitations of entry 15 to whole groups of applicants were
  removed by entry 31.
- **Rotation with a handover.** Once every 1 year / N the longest-serving super-node leaves without penalty — but only after its
  successor has passed the check and been included. Until then it keeps working.
- **Overload.** When deals per super-node exceed the overload constant, two new places open by the same choice. Once both are included,
  one super-node leaves: the one with the worst results in the period's checks under load — response time and refusals. There is no
  penalty. Each overload adds one super-node to the network.
- **The quality minimum stays.** A super-node that fails it for several periods leaves at any time, without penalty (entry 16; document
  06, section 6.2).
- **One description.** Places, rotation and overload are described in full in one place — document 05, section 5.6; other documents refer
  to it.

**Why.**
- Ten places give the network a minimum of independent servers from the first day, whatever the load: waiting for overload could leave a
  small network with a handful of super-nodes for a long time. Ten is also the first number at which no single operator holds more than a
  tenth of the places.
- A check after the invitation spares applicants the cost of keeping a server ready for a choice they may not get, and 48 hours keep a
  place from standing empty for long.
- A handover keeps capacity steady: in a small network the departure of a working super-node would cut it for the time a replacement
  needs.
- Under overload the network grows by one and replaces its weakest link, measured by the service it gave under load rather than by
  hardware on paper. The race for hardware that entry 15 rejected rewards declared power; a check under load measures what the network
  actually received.
- **The cost we do not hide.** Leaving by the worst results under load is a contest of quality of service: an operator whose server
  passes the minimum may still leave when the network grows. Who measures the load and the checks and how their results are combined is
  open (open questions).

**Documents:** 00, 05, 06, 15, 18, 22, README; README of `lagrix-supernode`.

## 41. The founder's public commitments

*Agreed on 15 October. New. These are commitments of the founder as a person, not rules of the contracts: the protocol treats the founder
like anyone (entry 18).*

**Before.** The documents said that the founder has no share and takes part only on the same terms as anyone (entry 18). They did not say what the founder does at the start, when the founder may be one of very few participants.

**Now.** At the launch of the network the founder:
- **runs one node and names it.** The founder publishes the wallet address of their node and states that it is their only node. Any
  further node or super-node of the founder is added to the same public list on the day it starts.
- **makes no deals with themselves.** The founder's elements do not order work from each other: the founder's node takes deals only with
  other people's elements.
- **takes no share of the fees while the network is small.** Until ten super-nodes have passed the quality minimum in a period, the
  founder takes no share of the fees: it stays in the pool of the other super-nodes. From the next period on, the founder takes part like
  anyone.

**Why.** With no founder share (entry 18), the one advantage left to a founder is time: whoever starts the network is for a while one of
few super-nodes and would take a large share of the equal parts. Ten is the first number at which no operator — the founder included —
holds more than a tenth of the places (entry 40). A commitment with a named address can be checked by anyone in the chain; a rule in the
contracts would have to tell the founder's nodes from others', which a contract cannot do. Entry 18 needs no named addresses, because the
contracts treat the founder like anyone; the founder names them anyway, so that these commitments can be checked. The share stays in
the pool of the other super-nodes, so nobody decides where it goes.

## 42. Fees, reputation and refunds

*Agreed on 15 October. Changes entries 25, 26 and 41.*
*Revised by entry 46: «burned» is no longer an indicator — a refund is deducted from the customer's likes.*

**Before.** A review weighed the network part of the deal divided by 10 (entry 26), and a customer who did not accept delivered work had
no way to cancel the order (document 04).

**Now.**
- **The network's money is USDC:** deal escrow, the 5% fee on the network part (entry 25), the $0.01 search fee (entry 39) and the $0.001
  fee per delivered H2H message (entry 33). Everyone pays their own gas (entries 37, 38). The fee is taken from the first deal and divided
  under entry 25.
- **A super-node's income is in USDC:** a share of the period's fees, split equally among super-nodes that passed the quality minimum
  (entry 25), plus search and delivery fees. A super-node place is earned by paid work (entries 31 and 40, document 05, section 5.6).
- **Reputation answers for a super-node's honesty.** A proven violation does not erase its history: a permanent, visible «proven
  violation» mark is added to the node's wallet and to all its elements, the node loses its place and may not hold places again. A new
  element starts from zero, and its way to a place is the same — paid work. Whoever proves a violation is rewarded in USDC from the fees;
  the amount is set by modelling.
- **A review weighs the fee paid on that deal, in USDC.** A $100 deal gives a review weighing $5. If a customer cancels a paid order, the
  refund is at most the weight of the customer's likes, and the same weight of likes burns; the provider receives the rest, and a
  "cancelled order" is recorded for both. Burning is a new event: history is not rewritten (entry 26), and the card shows "earned ·
  burned · cancelled orders" (entry 43).
- **The founder's commitment (entry 41):** until ten super-nodes pass the quality minimum in a period, the founder takes no share of the
  fees — it stays in the pool of the other super-nodes.
- **The licence is MIT from 15 October 2026.**
- Document 05 is called "Fees and reputation": the fee, the escrow and prepaid deposits, super-node places and reputation.

**Why.** The network has two goals — discovery and honest reputation (entry 20): searching, paying and rating work all happen in USDC,
and reputation weighs only what was paid for. A review weighs what the network received for the deal: faking $1 of weight costs exactly
$1 of fees, so the right to a refund can never be bought for less than the refund itself. A place takes a year of paid work to earn, and
a proven violation loses it for good with a mark nobody can erase — that is what a super-node has at stake. The cost is stated: while
deals are few, fees may not cover super-node servers — modelling checks this.

**Documents:** README, 00, 04, 05, 06, 07, 09, 15, 17, 18, 22; LICENSE; lagrix.dev.

## 43. Reputation: count and sum; "unreviewed"

*Agreed on 15 October. Extends entries 26 and 42.*
*Revised by entry 46: «burned» is no longer an indicator, and «negative ratings» are added.*

**Before.** In each role an element's reputation was two numbers: the weight of likes and the weight of dislikes (entry 26). How many
reviews stood behind a weight, and whether the element itself reviewed its counterparties, could not be seen.

**Now.**
- Every reputation indicator is a pair, **"count · sum"**, kept separately for each role. There are five indicators:
  - **likes** and **dislikes** — the number of reviews and their weight (fee paid, entry 42);
  - **burned** — the number of refunds that consumed likes, and the weight burned (entry 42);
  - **cancelled orders** — their number and the sum of their network parts;
  - **unreviewed** — deals in which the element could have reviewed its counterparty and did not: their number and the sum of their
    network parts.
- A review has no deadline (document 17, section 17.5): once given — a like or a dislike — it immediately removes the deal from
  "unreviewed". Only the element itself can change this indicator.
- A count is never shown without its sum. The rules of entry 26 stand: indicators are never combined into one score and do not affect
  search placement; the counterparty decides.

**Why.** A sum without a count hides what it is made of: one large deal and a hundred small ones look the same, and a thousand likes
weighing 50 cents in total reveal farming through tiny deals — only the pair shows it. "Unreviewed" serves both sides. The element sees
how much of its work with others it has left unrated. A counterparty sees that a deal with this element will probably not bring a review,
and so no reputation weight. A super-node place does not depend on it: it is earned by paid work (entry 31), which counts with or without
a review.

**Documents:** README, 07, 17; lagrix.dev.

## 44. Reputation: all orders and the proven-violation mark

*Agreed on 15 October. Extends entry 43.*

**Before.** An element's reputation in each role had five indicators (entry 43). How many orders the element had in that role could not
be seen, and document 17 kept the number of deals out of reputation as cheap to inflate. The «proven violation» mark of a super-node
(document 06) was shown next to the indicators without being named as part of what the counterparty sees.

**Now.**
- A sixth indicator comes first: **all orders** — the number of the element's paid orders in this role, completed or cancelled, and the

- **A proven violation as a super-node** is part of reputation: if the node's wallet carries the mark, every element of the node shows it

- The rules of entries 26 and 43 stand: the indicators are never combined into one score and do not affect search placement.

**Why.** The other indicators only make sense against the whole: three cancelled orders out of three hundred and three out of four are
different stories, and «unreviewed» means little without the number of orders behind it. A count alone is cheap to inflate, but the sum
next to it is not: every order in it paid the fee. Nothing in an element's record is hidden from the counterparty.

## 45. Super-node requirements grow with the network

*Agreed on 15 October. Extends entries 16, 30 and 40.*

**Before.** A super-node had to pass the quality minimum each period; one that failed for several periods left (entry 16), and under
overload the one with the worst results under load left (entry 40). Nothing said that the requirements grow, and an operator learned of
a shortfall only by failing.

**Now.**
- **Requirements grow.** The quality minimum is checked against the network as it is, so the hardware that was enough when a super-node
  joined may fall short as the network grows. The profiles of document 14 are a starting point, not a promise.
- **A warning first.** When the period's checks show that a super-node is short of capacity, its operator receives a warning at its point
  of contact (entry 32). The operator adds capacity within the deadline, and the super-node is checked again.
- **An early exit.** A super-node whose operator does not add capacity in time leaves its place early. This is not a penalty: there is no
  mark, and the place reopens by the rules of document 05, section 5.6.

**Why.** A network that grows needs super-nodes that grow with it. A warning gives an honest operator time to act before the checks
decide for them, and the early exit keeps a weak server from holding a place that someone else would serve better. Who sends the
warning, what «short of capacity» means and the length of the deadline are open (open questions).

## 46. Reputation: negative ratings; no «burned» indicator

*Agreed on 16 October. Revises entries 42 and 43.*

**Before.** In each role an element showed six indicators (entries 43 and 44). Two of them told how others rated it — likes and
dislikes; two told how it behaves itself — cancelled orders and unreviewed deals. How the element itself rates its partners could not be
seen. A refund paid with likes was shown as a separate indicator, «burned», next to the likes it was taken from.

**Now.**
- **Negative ratings** are a new indicator in each role: the share of the element's orders, by weight, that ended with its own dislike.
  It is the weight of the dislikes the element gave in this role divided by the weight of all its orders in this role in which it could
  leave a review, shown as a percent. The weight is the fee paid on each deal, the same as a review's weight (entry 42).
- Next to the percent stand the count and the weight of those dislikes: a count is never shown without its sum (entry 43), and 100% of
  one deal must not look like 100% of a hundred.
- The base is every order in which the element could leave a review, not only the ones it reviewed. The percent therefore tells a
  counterparty how likely a deal with this element is to end with its dislike; the deals it left silent are shown separately in
  «unreviewed».
- **«Burned» is no longer an indicator.** A cancellation still refunds at most the weight of the customer's likes as a customer, and that
  weight is now deducted from its likes as a new event with its date: the likes indicator shows the weight that remains. Reviews are never
  rewritten (entry 26), and what the customer cancelled stays visible in «cancelled orders».
- The indicators in each role are now: all orders, likes, dislikes, negative ratings, cancelled orders and unreviewed deals, with the
  proven-violation mark next to them. The rules of entry 26 stand: they are never combined into one score and do not affect search
  placement. Negative ratings are not such a score: they describe only the reviews the element gave.

**Why.** An element's record answers two questions: how others rated it, and how it behaves itself. A counterparty could see that an
element cancels orders and leaves deals unreviewed, but not how often it gives its partners a dislike — and that is what tells it in
advance how its own work is likely to be rated. The percent follows weight, not count: a thousand tiny deals with oneself would dilute a
count almost for free, while diluting weight costs real fees. A separate «burned» line had to be read against the likes it came from;
the weight of likes that remains says the same in one number, and «cancelled orders» keeps the history. The cost is stated: an element
that honestly dislikes poor partners shows a higher percent, and some may prefer silence to a dislike — which then shows in
«unreviewed». Both are visible, and the counterparty decides.

**Documents:** README, 00, 04, 07, 17, 18; lagrix.dev.

## 47. Principles of growth

*Agreed on 17 October. Extends entries 40 and 45 and document 18.*

**Before.** The rules mixed constants and formulas without saying which values must change as the network grows. Nothing kept a rule
from making the work of every super-node grow with the size of the whole network, overload was counted by deals alone (entry 40), and
nothing said what happens when the network reaches a limit that no rule can move.

**Now.** Five principles. Every later rule is checked against them.
- **A formula, not a number, for whatever depends on size.** A value that depends on the size of the network is a formula of what the
  contracts see — receipts, redeemed search and delivery vouchers, the registry of super-nodes. Bitcoin works this way: every 2016 blocks
  it recomputes its difficulty so that a block still comes about every ten minutes, whatever the computing power of the network.
  Ethereum moves its base fee every block towards a target (EIP-1559). The values that express the mission — the fee ceiling, the price
  of a search, the price of a delivered message — stay constants.
- **The work of one super-node stays bounded.** No rule may make each super-node do work in proportion to the number of all agents or
  all super-nodes. Work that grows with the network is divided into parts that anyone can compute from the registry, and the network
  meets growth by adding super-nodes. What one super-node must carry may still rise (entry 45), but not in proportion to the whole
  network.
- **Overload counts all the work the contracts see.** New places open under overload (entry 40) when deals, redeemed searches or
  delivered messages per super-node exceed their constants, not deals alone.
- **Few transactions per deal.** Whatever can be settled in a batch is settled in a batch, as search vouchers already are: the
  throughput of the blockchain is a limit that no formula in a contract can move.
- **A limit no formula moves is passed by a new version.** When the network reaches such a limit — the throughput of one blockchain
  network above all — it grows through a new version of the contracts, possibly on several networks, which participants adopt of their
  own will (document 18, section 18.3). Reputation stays bound to the element.

**Why.** The network is meant to serve billions of agents years from now, on hardware nobody can measure today. A model of that load
built on today's machines would prove nothing. What can be checked today is the shape of the rules: whether the work of each super-node
stays bounded as the network grows. An unchanging rule can serve a network many orders of magnitude larger than at its start when it
adapts by formula, as Bitcoin's difficulty shows. Modelling therefore checks every rule as the load on one super-node as a function of
the size of the network, not as the capacity of a given year.

**Documents:** 05, 18; README; README of `lagrix-supernode`.

## 48. Announcements reach the super-nodes that hold them; a new super-node catches up

*Agreed on 17 October. Extends entries 30, 32 and 40; applies entry 47.*

**Before.** Announcements travelled over Nostr. Every super-node ran its own relay or subscribed to relays, gathered announcements into
its own index and declared which relays it read (entry 32, documents 17 and 18). Nothing said where a node should publish so that the
super-nodes see its agents, how a super-node learns the relays of the others, how a new super-node fills its empty index, or whether
the completeness of an index is checked. A super-node that was missing an agent could always say it had never received the
announcement, and section 17.3, which forbids dropping one agent inside a declared kind, had nothing to compare against.

**Now.**
- **Every super-node runs a relay, recorded with its place.** When a super-node registers its place in SuperNodeRegistry, it records the
  address of its Nostr relay; only the wallet that holds the place can change it. The relay's information document (NIP-11) carries the
  super-node's declaration (document 18, section 18.4). The list of super-nodes and their relays is read from the contract; nobody keeps
  a list of their own.
- **Each kind of service has holders that anyone can compute.** A kind of service — the capability (tag `c`) a customer searches
  for — is held by the k super-nodes whose keys are closest to the key of that kind of service, counting only super-nodes whose declared
  restrictions do not exclude it
  (section 17.9); k is a constant. Anyone computes the holders from the registry and the declarations; nobody assigns them. While no more
  than k super-nodes cover a kind, all of them hold it. As super-nodes join, each holds a smaller share of all kinds, and no rule has to
  change.
- **Any super-node may hold more.** Beyond its duty, a super-node may add any kind to its coverage. The search fee goes to the super-node
  that built the result list (section 17.8), so the kinds people search for most draw more holders by themselves.
- **A node publishes to the holders of its kinds.** A node publishes each announcement and each update to the relays of the holders of
  its kinds, and may add any other relay. Publishing costs nothing: a super-node's relay takes announcements free of charge.
- **Holders copy from each other.** Every holder keeps a subscription (NIP-01) to the relays of the other holders of the same kind,
  checks the author's signature on each announcement and keeps it on its own relay. An announcement that reached one holder reaches the
  others, even if its node has gone offline since. A super-node never copies from all super-nodes, only from the holders of its kinds.
- **A new super-node catches up before its check.** Before it submits its server for the check (document 05, section 5.6), a new
  super-node takes from the other holders every stored announcement of the kinds it will hold — by Negentropy set reconciliation (NIP-77)
  where the other relay supports it, so that only the missing events travel — checks the signatures and builds its index; it reads
  receipts and reviews from the blockchain. A super-node coming back after downtime does the same from the moment it last saw (the
  `since` filter of NIP-01). It copies signed announcements, never another super-node's index or result list, so it has to trust
  nobody: an announcement that was forged or altered fails its signature.
- **The index is checked.** The checks by agents in turn — at entry and for the quality minimum of every period — include the index: an
  announcement that has been on the relays of the other holders for longer than the propagation time must appear in this super-node's
  results when it matches the query and lies within its coverage. A super-node whose index misses such announcements fails the check; as
  with downtime, it loses income and, if it keeps failing, its place, without a mark. Dropping one agent inside a declared kind remains a
  violation (section 17.3).
- **Announcements expire.** Every announcement carries an expiration (NIP-40) no further ahead than a fixed period; a node renews its
  announcements while its agents work, and relays and indexes drop expired ones. Storage therefore follows the active agents and their
  services — one latest version for each «agent key + kind + `d` tag» (NIP-01) — not the history of updates and not agents that have
  left.

**Why.** Discovery is one of the network's two goals (entry 20), and it cannot depend on which relays an agent happened to know. With the
relay address in the contract and the holders computed from it, every node and every newcomer finds the right relays without any central
list, so no single point of failure appears. A full copy of every announcement on every super-node, copied from every other super-node,
would make each super-node's work grow with the whole network — exactly what entry 47 forbids; holders keep that work bounded, and the
search fee spreads the busy kinds. A search for one kind is answered by one holder, which keeps the whole kind, so the ranking of section
17.2 and the turn of section 17.3 stay as they are. «I never received it» is no longer an answer: an announcement on the other holders'
relays is expected in every index that holds its kind. The cost is stated: a node publishes each update to at least k super-nodes; a
holder keeps a subscription to the other holders of each of its kinds; a newcomer's catch-up grows with the size of the kinds it takes on.
The constant k, the propagation time, the longest expiration and what happens to a kind too large for one server are open (open
questions).

## Contradictions resolved

- **The delivery deadline and the dispute window** (document 04, README glossary, lagrix.dev). The words «acceptance window» named two
  different deadlines: in document 04 the time by which the provider must deliver (no delivery — the money returns to the customer), in
  the glossary the time in which the customer must answer after delivery (no answer — the money goes to the provider). Now the first is
  the «delivery deadline», the second the «dispute window», and «acceptance» remains the customer's act.
- **Downtime is not penalised** (document 06). Document 16 described downtime as leading to `slashValidator()` and "Jail"; it now
  follows 06.
- **The network does not depend on our domain.** `paymaster.lagrix.dev` (documents 12, 13) and `get.lagrix.dev` (document 15) are
  removed: the paymaster is a contract address, and the operator's tools come from the repository's releases.
- **Discovery runs through Nostr and the super-node's index** (document 17). Documents 12 and 13 described a separate Kademlia service
  registry; they now follow 17. The SDK remembers a provider it has found and does not search again for every task.
- **A vacancy reopens on the terms of the day it reopens** (entries 15, 16). Documents 05 and 06 said that a vacated place
  reopens on the terms on which it was taken; they now follow this changelog.

## Open questions

These are not decided in this revision and are marked where they appear. The entry of super-nodes beyond the first 100 and the
live-agent check, open in the first part of this revision, are answered by entries 15 and 16.

- **How "passed or failed" is measured.** Entry 30 says agents taken in turn check each super-node against a quality minimum;
  what they measure, how the turn is kept and how their results are combined is not decided. The same holds for the overload of the
  network and for the checks under load that decide which super-node leaves (entry 40), and for the capacity warning: who sends it, what «short»
  means and the deadline (entry 45), and for the check of the index (entry 48).
- **The `superNode` field of a deal.** The customer names the super-node that receives a deal's receipt (document 04); nothing yet ties
  it to the node that actually served the deal.
- **Payment for relaying traffic.** Earlier documents disagreed — in USDC per megabyte or none at all. For H2H messages it is
  decided by entry 33 ($0.001 per delivered message); other relayed traffic is still described without a price.
- **The time slot of the search turn** (entry 30): its length is set by modelling.
- **The holders of a kind and the life of an announcement** (entry 48): the constant k, how long an announcement may take to reach the
  other holders before its absence counts against an index, how far ahead an expiration may be set, how the key of a kind is computed,
  and what happens to a kind too large for one server, are set by modelling and at implementation.
- **The thresholds of overload** (entry 47): the constants of deals, redeemed searches and delivered messages per super-node above which
  new places open are set by modelling.
- **How the contract finds the median cheaply** (entry 31): recomputing over all nodes for every open place is expensive; the method is
  not chosen.
- **Collusion at reveal** (entry 31): the last revealer can withhold its secret and so change the result — it cannot help itself this
  way, but it can help an accomplice. How this is penalised is not decided.
- **The names of layer 1 tags and the format of a signed restriction list** (entry 32): chosen at implementation.
- **The reward to whoever proves a violation** (entry 42): its size, paid in USDC from the fees, is set by modelling.
- **Whether the fees pay for super-node servers while the network is small** (entry 42): checked by modelling.
- **A cancelled order** (entry 42): whether the fee is taken from the part the provider receives, and whether a cancelled order leaves a
  receipt and reviews, is not decided.
